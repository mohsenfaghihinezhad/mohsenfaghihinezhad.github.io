Teaching Philosophy

Connecting Fundamentals to Meaningful Problems

My own interest in Environmental Engineering developed from seeing how chemical and biological processes can be used to address contaminants that conventional treatment does not remove. Work ranging from catalytic ozonation to microbial biodegradation showed me that environmental problems rarely fit within a single subdiscipline and that technically sound decisions must also account for public health, sustainability, cost, and community needs. This experience shapes my teaching philosophy: I want students to learn to think like engineers by connecting fundamentals across systems, asking why a technology succeeds or fails, and using evidence to justify practical decisions. Although my examples often come from Environmental Engineering, these habits—systems thinking, quantitative reasoning, communication, and evaluation of tradeoffs—are central to engineering education more broadly.

I believe students learn more deeply when they understand why a concept matters before they are asked to master its details. I therefore create a sense of “need to know” by beginning topics with questions drawn from current practice. For example, I might ask: How can a treatment plant meet conventional standards while trace contaminants remain in the discharged water? Students can then use mass balances, reaction kinetics, transport, and process design to compare conventional treatment, advanced oxidation, adsorption, and biological approaches. They must consider not only removal efficiency, but also energy demand, by-products, cost, and effects on surrounding communities. Framing a lesson this way turns technical material into a problem students want to solve and demonstrates that Environmental Engineering is a changing field in which established methods must continually be evaluated and improved.

Active and Collaborative Learning

I combine structured explanation with frequent opportunities to retrieve, apply, and discuss ideas. Short lectures establish essential concepts; low-stakes check-ins, think-pair-share questions, annotated process diagrams, and case-based activities allow students to test their understanding. Rather than asking only which method is correct, I ask students to explain the assumptions behind their choice and identify the conditions under which another approach might be preferable. This helps them move beyond memorization toward flexible problem solving.

Collaborative learning is also important because engineering decisions are rarely made from a single perspective. In small-group cases, students may take on different responsibilities—such as process performance, cost, sustainability, or community impact—and then combine their analyses into a recommendation. This structure gives each student a meaningful role, exposes the class to multiple viewpoints, and develops the communication skills needed in professional practice.

Assessment for Reasoning and Growth

My assessments are aligned with the same reasoning students practice in class. Homework develops technical fluency, while exams ask students to connect concepts and explain their decisions. Case-based assignments require students to interpret realistic information, state assumptions, compare alternatives, and justify a recommendation. When possible, I use staged submissions or brief reflections so students can identify what changed in their reasoning after feedback or peer discussion. These opportunities make assessment part of the learning process rather than only a measure of performance. They also help me identify recurring misconceptions and adjust instruction, examples, or pacing.

Inclusive and Purposeful Teaching

Effective teaching requires both high expectations and multiple pathways for students to participate. Students enter engineering courses with different backgrounds, prior preparation, and confidence. I make expectations transparent through clear objectives, examples of successful work, and criteria for evaluation. I also use a mix of individual writing, anonymous or low-stakes responses, small-group discussion, and whole-class conversation so that participation does not depend on immediate public speaking. Beginning with individual thinking time before discussion helps more students formulate ideas and contribute. I normalize questions and revisions by treating uncertainty and mistakes as part of engineering problem solving, and I invite students to explain different approaches rather than rewarding only the fastest answer.

Examples and case studies should also reflect the range of communities affected by environmental decisions. By asking who benefits, who bears risk, and what constraints shape implementation, I help students see that engineering solutions are situated within social and ethical contexts. This supports belonging while preparing students to make responsible professional decisions.

I use technology when it makes an idea more visible or participation more accessible. Process-flow diagrams, reaction pathways, animations, and schematics can help students connect equations to physical systems. Digital polling or shared-response tools can reveal patterns in student thinking quickly and allow me to address misunderstandings before moving forward. Spreadsheet-based calculations and simple modeling activities can show how changes in design parameters affect system performance. Course platforms provide organized access to materials and feedback, while modern analytical and computational tools introduce students to practices used in current engineering research and employment. The tool is never the objective; I select it according to the learning problem it helps solve.

My goal is to create a rigorous, inclusive, and engaging learning environment in which students connect fundamentals to consequential problems. By aligning objectives, instruction, and assessment, I want students to leave my courses able to analyze complex systems, justify decisions, collaborate across perspectives, and continue learning as engineering challenges evolve. Most importantly, I want them to recognize that Environmental Engineering is not only a set of calculations, but a way of using evidence and creativity to protect public health, improve environmental quality, and serve communities.
